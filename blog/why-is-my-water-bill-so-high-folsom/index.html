Why Is My Water Bill Suddenly So High?

If your bill jumped and nothing in your routine changed, the most likely explanation is a leak you haven't found yet, not a billing error.

By the Folsom Express Plumbing team

A water bill that climbs for no obvious reason is one of the most common things that brings Folsom homeowners to a plumber, and it's rarely a billing mistake. Water utilities don't usually make errors that produce a sustained increase over multiple billing cycles, what's far more common is a leak somewhere in the system that's running continuously without ever producing an obvious puddle.

The first, simplest check costs nothing and takes two minutes: turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water in your house, then go look at your water meter. Most meters have a small triangular or circular dial, sometimes called a leak indicator, that spins whenever water is flowing through the line. If everything in the house is off and that indicator is still moving, water is going somewhere, and it's worth investigating before the next bill arrives even higher.

Toilets are the single most common source of a silent leak that drives up a bill without anyone noticing. A worn flapper can let water seep from the tank into the bowl continuously, refilling the tank over and over throughout the day and night. A simple test: add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 10 minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. It's an inexpensive part to replace and one of the most common repairs we make.

If the toilets check out and the meter still moves with everything off, the next suspects are outdoor: an irrigation system with a cracked line, a pool or spa with a slow leak, or a hose bib that doesn't fully shut off. These are easy to overlook because they're outside the house entirely, and the water often soaks into the ground rather than pooling visibly. Soil can absorb a surprising amount of water, so a meaningful leak can run for weeks before the ground even looks noticeably wet.

A less common but more serious possibility is an underground leak in the service line between the meter and the house, or a slab leak under the foundation itself. These don't show up as an obvious wet spot right away, sometimes the first sign is a warm patch on the floor if it's a hot water line, or a section of unusually green grass along the water line's path. If you've ruled out toilets and obvious outdoor sources and the meter is still moving, this is when electronic leak detection becomes worth calling in, rather than guessing and opening walls speculatively.

One more thing worth knowing: municipal water rates sometimes increase between billing cycles. A genuine change in household water use, guests staying longer, a garden watered more during a hot stretch, filling a pool, can also explain a real increase that has nothing to do with a leak. Ruling those out first with an honest look at your own routine saves you from chasing a leak that doesn't exist.

It's also worth understanding how your bill is actually calculated, since that context helps you judge whether an increase is proportional to a real leak or something smaller. Water utilities bill in units such as CCF (hundred cubic feet) or gallons, and your bill states which one. A single running toilet flapper can waste somewhere in the range of 200 gallons a day, which adds up to a genuinely large number by the end of a billing cycle. A slab leak or a failed irrigation valve can waste substantially more than that. That's part of why the size of the bill increase is itself a useful clue: a modest jump often points to a fixture-level issue, while a dramatic jump points toward something larger like a service line or slab leak.

If you've gone through every check above and still can't find an obvious source, that's exactly the point where guessing further stops being useful. Opening walls or digging up a yard speculatively, hoping to get lucky, wastes time and money and often causes damage that didn't need to happen. Electronic leak detection equipment can pinpoint a hidden leak's location acoustically or thermally without any of that guesswork, and it's a far more direct path from a mysterious high bill to an actual repair. Any repair we make afterward follows California Plumbing Code (UPC-based) standards regardless of how small the fix turns out to be.

One pattern worth flagging for homes built on a slab: soil that swells and shrinks with the seasons can stress pipes under the concrete, and that is a recognized contributor to slab leaks in older and newer homes alike. If your high bill shows up alongside a warm patch on the floor, especially in a newer slab-foundation home, that combination is a strong signal worth acting on quickly rather than waiting for the next bill to confirm it wasn't a fluke.

Keeping a simple record of your usage over time makes future comparisons much easier. Most water utilities let you view usage history online. Glancing at it every few months, even just noting whether this quarter looks roughly like the same quarter last year, means you'll catch a real leak faster the next time one develops rather than discovering it three billing cycles later.

What to do before calling a plumber about a high water bill

  • Turn off every fixture and check whether the meter's leak indicator is still moving
  • Test every toilet in the house with the food-coloring method
  • Check outdoor spigots, irrigation lines, and pool or spa equipment for visible dripping
  • Compare this bill's usage (in gallons or CCF) to the same month last year
  • Note any warm spots on flooring or unusually green patches in the yard

Checking your Folsom water meter and usage history

The City of Folsom says your water meter is typically near one of your side property lines, close to the sidewalk, in a concrete box. Keep the lid clear and avoid planting shrubs or trees near it, because roots can cause problems with the underground water pipes.

To see your consumption history, the City points customers to DropCountr, a Folsom water customer portal. To report a water leak, call the City at 916-461-6177 during normal business hours (7 a.m. to 3:30 p.m.). For water emergencies after hours, call 916-461-6400.
