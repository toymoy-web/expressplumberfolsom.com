Buying an Older Home in Folsom? Here's What to Check Plumbing-Wise

A general home inspection covers a lot of ground quickly. These are the plumbing-specific questions worth asking directly.

By the Folsom Express Plumbing team

A standard home inspection is a broad, general assessment covering the whole property in a limited amount of time, and plumbing often gets a surface-level look rather than the kind of detailed assessment a specialist would provide. If you're buying an older home in Folsom, particularly anything in or near the Historic District, there are specific plumbing questions worth asking directly rather than assuming the general inspection covered everything relevant.

Supply line material is the first thing to ask about, and it's not always obvious from a visual inspection alone. Homes built before the 1970s commonly have original galvanized steel supply lines, which corrode from the inside in a way that's invisible from the outside, meaning a pipe can look fine during inspection while already being significantly narrowed by internal rust. If the inspection report doesn't specify supply line material, ask directly, and consider a plumbing-specific assessment if the home's age suggests galvanized pipe is likely.

Sewer lateral condition is the second major question, and it's genuinely worth a dedicated camera inspection rather than relying on a general inspector's assessment, since a sewer camera inspection isn't standard practice for most general home inspections. Older clay sewer laterals are prone to root intrusion and joint separation that won't show up as a current problem during a quick inspection but represents a real, foreseeable expense if left unaddressed. A camera inspection before closing, or as a contingency, gives you actual information rather than a guess.

Water heater age and condition matters directly since it's a predictable, bounded expense if replacement is needed soon. Check the manufacture date on the unit's label, most units display it clearly, and compare that against typical lifespans: 10 to 12 years for a tank unit, up to 20 for a well-maintained tankless system. A unit near or past its expected lifespan isn't a dealbreaker, but it's a known, near-term cost worth factoring into your overall budget or negotiation.

Water pressure and general system function are worth checking in person during a walkthrough, beyond what the formal inspection report states. Run multiple fixtures at once if possible, a shower and a sink, or a washing machine and a shower, and see whether pressure drops noticeably. This kind of real-world test can reveal pressure issues from aging or undersized pipe that a static, single-fixture inspection might miss entirely.

Foundation type affects what kind of plumbing issues are more or less likely going forward. A slab-foundation home has supply and sometimes drain lines running under the concrete, which means future leaks in those specific lines require more invasive access than a raised foundation or basement would. This isn't a reason to avoid a slab-foundation home, most handle this fine for decades, but it's useful context for understanding what a future plumbing issue in that specific home might actually involve.

If any of these questions raise concerns, or if the seller's disclosures mention past plumbing work, a repiping, a sewer repair, a slab leak, it's worth getting a full plumbing assessment before closing rather than after. We can provide an independent assessment covering supply lines, sewer condition, water heater status, and overall system function, giving you real information to negotiate with or simply enter the purchase with clear eyes about what you're taking on.

It's worth putting the cost of a pre-purchase plumbing assessment in perspective against what you're actually buying. A few hundred dollars spent confirming pipe material, sewer condition, and water heater status is a small fraction of what a surprise repipe or sewer repair would cost after closing. It gives you either real peace of mind or genuine negotiating leverage, both of which are worth having before you're financially committed to the property.

One pattern worth watching for specifically in Historic Folsom listings: a home that's had recent cosmetic updates, new flooring, fresh paint, updated fixtures, can sometimes mask rather than reflect the condition of what's actually inside the walls. A beautifully renovated kitchen doesn't tell you anything about the supply line feeding it, and it's worth asking directly whether any plumbing work was part of the renovation or whether the update was purely cosmetic, since sellers aren't always volunteering that distinction unprompted.

If you're working with a real estate agent unfamiliar with the specific plumbing patterns common to Folsom's different neighborhoods, it can help to share what you've learned here. That's particularly true of the distinction between older Historic District infrastructure and newer slab-foundation construction, since not every agent's general experience covers this level of local, material-specific detail. An agent who understands the difference can help you ask sharper questions of the seller and set more realistic expectations during negotiation, rather than treating every older home in the area as an identical risk profile.

Timing your assessment matters too. Scheduling it during your standard inspection contingency period, rather than after you've already waived contingencies to make an offer more competitive, preserves your ability to actually act on whatever the assessment finds, whether that means renegotiating price, requesting repairs, or in a worst case, walking away from a property with more significant problems than the listing suggested.

Plumbing questions to ask before buying an older Folsom home

  • What material are the supply lines, and has the home ever been repiped?
  • Has the sewer lateral ever had a camera inspection, and what did it show?
  • What's the water heater's age, and has it had any past issues?
  • Does water pressure hold up when multiple fixtures run at once?
  • Do the seller's disclosures mention any past plumbing repairs or leaks?
