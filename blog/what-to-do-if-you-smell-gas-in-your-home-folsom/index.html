What to Do If You Smell Gas in Your Home

There's no ambiguity here: leave first, ask questions after. Here's the correct order of steps.

By the Folsom Express Plumbing team

Natural gas is naturally odorless, which is why utilities add a distinctive rotten-egg-smelling compound called mercaptan specifically so a leak is noticeable before it becomes a serious hazard. If you smell that odor in your home, the response isn't complicated, but it does need to happen in the right order and without hesitation.

Leave the house immediately. Don't stop to open windows, don't turn off appliances, don't investigate where the smell is coming from. Any of these actions, along with using a light switch, a phone, or any other electrical device, carries a small but real risk of creating a spark that could ignite accumulated gas. The safest action is simply to get everyone out of the house as quickly and calmly as possible.

Once you're safely outside and away from the house, at least a reasonable distance, not just standing right outside the front door, call your gas utility's emergency line. Utilities maintain a 24/7 emergency response specifically for this situation, and they'll dispatch someone to assess and, if necessary, shut off gas service to the property. Call us as well, since if the source turns out to be a plumbing-related connection rather than a utility-side issue, we're the ones who can actually fix it once it's confirmed safe to do so.

Don't attempt to locate the leak yourself, and don't go back inside to grab anything you forgot, even something that feels urgent, until a professional has confirmed it's safe to re-enter. A gas leak large enough to be smelled from outside the house, or one that continues to build in an enclosed space, represents a real explosion and fire risk that isn't worth any object inside the home.

Once the immediate danger is addressed and the property is confirmed safe, the actual leak gets diagnosed and repaired. Leaks at the plumbing level most often originate at connection points rather than mid-pipe, a fitting that's loosened over time, a flexible connector to an appliance that's degraded, or a valve that's failed to seal completely. We use a combustible gas detector to pinpoint the exact location rather than guessing based on where the smell seemed strongest, since gas travels and can pool in ways that don't always match its actual source.

It's worth knowing the difference between a strong, sudden gas smell and a very faint, intermittent one, since the response should be the same either way but the likely cause can differ. A strong, sudden smell often indicates a more significant leak, possibly at a major connection or supply line. A faint smell that comes and goes, sometimes only noticeable near a specific appliance like a stove or water heater, can indicate a smaller leak at that appliance's connection, still worth immediate attention, just potentially a smaller-scope repair once confirmed safe.

Prevention matters here as much as response. If you have gas appliances, having connections and lines checked periodically, especially older flexible connectors that degrade over time, catches a developing problem before it becomes an actual leak you can smell. This is something we check as a matter of course any time we're doing water heater or other gas appliance work, since a connection issue caught during unrelated service is far better than one discovered later as an active leak.

It's also worth having a working carbon monoxide detector installed near gas appliances and sleeping areas, separate from any gas leak concern specifically. A gas appliance that's venting improperly can produce carbon monoxide even without an odor-detectable gas leak occurring at the same time, and a CO detector catches a real, separate hazard that a person's nose alone cannot. If your home doesn't have one, it's a quick and inexpensive addition worth making, ideally installed at the same time as any other gas-related work.

For a family with children old enough to understand the concept, it's worth briefly explaining what to do if they ever notice the smell themselves, get an adult, leave the house, don't touch anything electrical. Making sure everyone in the household, not just adults, knows the basic response removes a dangerous gap in coverage if the person who first notices the smell happens to be a child home alone or the only person awake at the time.

If a gas leak has occurred and been resolved, it's reasonable to want a broader check of your home's gas appliances afterward rather than assuming the single repaired connection was the only vulnerable point. Appliances of a similar age installed around the same time as the one that failed sometimes share the same wear pattern. A full check gives you real confidence rather than a nagging uncertainty about whether something else might fail soon after the first repair.

It's also reasonable to feel some lingering anxiety after a real gas scare, even once everything has been repaired and confirmed safe. That reaction is normal, and it usually fades once you've seen the actual repair, understand what caused it, and know the appliance or connection has been checked and cleared. If the anxiety persists longer than feels reasonable to you, a follow-up visit just to walk through what was found and fixed, even without any remaining technical concern, is something we're glad to provide, all performed to California Plumbing Code (UPC-based) gas piping standards.

If you smell gas, in order

  • Leave the house immediately, don't touch switches or investigate the source
  • Get to a safe distance away from the property, not just outside the door
  • Call your gas utility's emergency line
  • Call us once you're safely away
  • Don't re-enter until a professional confirms it's safe

The numbers to call in Folsom

PG&E's guidance for a suspected gas leak is to leave the area immediately and call 911, then call its 24-hour line at 1-877-660-6789. PG&E lists a rotten-egg smell, hissing or whistling sounds, and damaged connections at appliances as signs of a possible leak.

If your gas comes from PG&E, those are the numbers that matter in an emergency. Call us afterward, from outside the house, so we can repair the plumbing-side cause once the utility says the area is safe.

We also serve nearby Rancho Cordova and Cameron Park, so help is close if you are in one of those areas.
