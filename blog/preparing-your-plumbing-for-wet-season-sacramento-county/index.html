Preparing Your Plumbing for Wet Season

A few checks before the rain starts can prevent the calls we get most often once it does.

By the Folsom Express Plumbing team

Sacramento County's climate runs long, dry summers followed by a genuinely wet winter, and that seasonal shift puts specific, predictable stress on plumbing systems that's worth preparing for rather than reacting to after the first storm. A little preparation each fall meaningfully reduces the odds of an emergency call once the rain actually arrives.

Sump pumps are the first thing worth checking if your home has one, particularly for properties in lower-lying areas or those near the American River corridor around Rancho Cordova and Gold River. A pump that hasn't run in months can fail silently, a stuck float switch, a clogged intake, or simply a worn-out motor, and the first sign of failure is often water in a crawlspace during the exact storm the pump exists to handle. Testing a sump pump by pouring a bucket of water into the pit and confirming it activates and drains properly takes a few minutes and catches most failures before they matter.

Battery backup for a sump pump deserves specific attention here, since the situations that cause sump pumps to be needed most, heavy storms, are also the situations most likely to cause a power outage. A pump with no battery backup does nothing during exactly the event it's meant to handle if the power goes out at the same time, which is a real and recurring pattern during significant Sacramento County storms, not a rare edge case.

Exterior drainage is worth a real look before the season starts. Gutters clogged with the previous season's leaves and debris can't move water away from your roof and foundation effectively, which sends more water toward your foundation than a clear gutter system would. Downspouts that discharge right next to the foundation rather than several feet away contribute to the same soil-saturation issues that stress underground pipes and can contribute to slab leaks over time, given Folsom's clay and sandy-loam soil composition.

Outdoor spigots and irrigation systems benefit from a check as well, even though winterizing them isn't as critical here as in colder climates. A slow leak at an outdoor spigot that goes unnoticed during dry summer months becomes more consequential during wet season when the ground is already saturated and less able to absorb additional water without pooling near the foundation.

Inside the house, a water heater inspection before the season's heavier usage begins, more showers, more hot water for laundry, is a reasonable preventive step, particularly for a unit that's approaching the end of its typical service life. A failure during a cold snap is a worse time to discover an aging unit's problems than during a routine fall check.

If your home has experienced any water intrusion in a crawlspace or low-lying area in a previous wet season, and you haven't addressed the underlying cause, this is the point in the year to deal with it, before the next storm rather than after it. Whether that means installing a sump pump for the first time, adding a battery backup to an existing one, or addressing a grading issue that's been sending water toward your foundation, tackling it now matters. It's considerably less stressful than doing it during an active flooding event.

It's worth thinking about wet-season preparation as a once-a-year habit rather than a one-time fix, since the same checks are worth repeating each fall regardless of whether last year's rain caused any actual problems. A sump pump that worked fine last winter can still fail this year if its float switch has started sticking or its motor has simply aged another year, and gutters that were clear last October need clearing again this year regardless of how well they performed previously.

For homeowners in Folsom's flatter, lower-elevation areas specifically, drainage swales and any French drain systems already installed on the property are worth a visual check before the season starts as well, confirming they're clear of debris and functioning as designed. A drainage system that's been quietly clogging over a dry summer won't show the problem until the first real storm actually tests it, at which point discovering the issue means dealing with active water intrusion rather than a preventable fix.

If last year's wet season brought any surprises, an unexpected sump pump failure, a slow drain that got noticeably worse during heavy rain, water finding its way into a crawlspace for the first time, treat those as data points rather than one-off bad luck. A pattern that repeats even loosely from one wet season to the next usually has a specific, addressable cause, and it's worth having it assessed properly before the pattern has a chance to repeat itself for a third consecutive year.

None of this preparation needs to happen all at once or cost significant money for most homes. A sump pump test, a gutter check, and a walk around the property looking for obvious drainage issues can realistically be done in an afternoon, and addressing whatever that walkthrough reveals is usually far less expensive than dealing with the same issue as an emergency once the rain has already started and the problem is actively causing damage inside the home. Any sump pump or drainage work we perform follows California Plumbing Code (UPC-based) standards.

What to do before wet season arrives

  • Test your sump pump by pouring water into the pit and confirming it activates
  • Confirm your sump pump has a working battery backup
  • Clear gutters and confirm downspouts discharge away from the foundation
  • Check outdoor spigots and irrigation lines for slow leaks
  • Have an aging water heater inspected before heavier winter usage begins
