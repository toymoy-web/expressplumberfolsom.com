How to Tell If You Have a Slab Leak

A slab leak rarely announces itself with a puddle. These are the quieter signs worth taking seriously.

By the Folsom Express Plumbing team

A slab leak is a leak in a water line running underneath your home's concrete foundation, and because the pipe is buried in concrete, the usual signs of a leak, visible water, a wet spot, don't show up the way they would with a leak inside a wall or under a sink. That's part of what makes slab leaks genuinely more concerning than most other plumbing issues: by the time they're obvious, real damage has often already occurred.

Folsom's newer neighborhoods, Broadstone, Empire Ranch, Natoma Station, and similar slab-foundation subdivisions, are more likely to have this specific risk simply because of how they're built. Homes on a raised foundation or with a crawlspace have supply lines that are at least somewhat accessible. A slab-foundation home runs its supply lines directly under or through the concrete, which means any leak there has nowhere to go but to travel along the slab and find whatever path of least resistance it can.

The most reliable early sign is a warm spot on your floor, usually over a hot water line, that's noticeably warmer than the surrounding flooring, sometimes enough to feel through socks or bare feet. This happens because hot water leaking under the slab transfers heat upward through the concrete. If you've noticed one spot in a room that's always a little warmer than the rest, especially near where you'd expect a water line to run toward a bathroom or kitchen, that's worth investigating rather than dismissing as your imagination.

A second reliable sign is the same one that shows up with any hidden leak: a water bill that's climbed without an obvious explanation. Since a slab leak runs continuously rather than intermittently, it tends to produce a more dramatic and sustained increase than a smaller fixture leak would, sometimes doubling or more depending on how severe the leak is and how long it's been running.

Other signs are more physical: cracks appearing in flooring, especially tile, that weren't there before, since water pooling under a slab can shift it slightly over time. A musty smell with no obvious source, particularly in a room over the leak's location, since moisture trapped under flooring has nowhere to properly dry. And in more advanced cases, the sound of running water when everything in the house is off, audible if you put your ear near the floor in a quiet room.

If you're noticing any combination of these signs, the right next step is electronic leak detection, not speculative demolition. Acoustic and thermal detection equipment can locate a slab leak's position accurately enough that any necessary opening of the slab is targeted rather than exploratory, which matters both for cost and for minimizing the disruption to your home. Once the leak is located, the repair approach depends on its position: sometimes a direct slab penetration is the right call, sometimes rerouting the line through an accessible wall or attic space avoids the slab entirely. Either approach is performed to California Plumbing Code (UPC-based) standards.

The sooner a slab leak is addressed, the less collateral damage it causes. Left alone, continued water intrusion can affect flooring, drywall, and in serious cases the foundation itself, turning what starts as a plumbing repair into a much larger and more expensive restoration project. A warm spot or an unexplained bill increase is worth a call well before it progresses to visible cracking or standing water.

It's worth understanding why slab leaks specifically tend to happen in the first place, since the cause affects what preventive steps actually help. Ground movement from Folsom's clay and sandy-loam soil, which expands and contracts seasonally with the area's wet winters and dry summers, puts real mechanical stress on any pipe running through or under a slab. Over years, that repeated stress can fatigue a pipe at joints or bends, eventually producing a leak even in a system that was installed correctly to begin with. This isn't a sign of poor construction, it's a predictable consequence of the region's soil combined with enough time.

Copper pipe under a slab, common in homes built a couple of decades ago, is also susceptible to a specific failure mode called pitting corrosion, small, localized points of corrosion that can perforate a pipe wall while the surrounding pipe looks completely fine. This is part of why two homes built around the same time, using the same materials, can have very different slab-leak histories, water chemistry, soil contact, and even how the original installation was done all play a role in which specific spots become vulnerable first.

If a slab leak is confirmed and repair is needed, we'll walk through the real options rather than defaulting to the most invasive one. A localized slab penetration to access and fix the specific leaking section is often the most cost-effective approach when the leak is isolated. Rerouting the line entirely, running a new supply line through the attic or an accessible wall cavity instead of through the slab, avoids cutting the slab at all, which can be worth the extra material cost if it means avoiding flooring repair and finish work afterward. We'll quote both when both are realistic options for your situation.

Signs of a possible slab leak

  • A warm spot on the floor, especially over where a hot water line likely runs
  • Water bill increased noticeably with no clear explanation
  • New cracks in tile or flooring
  • A musty smell in one room with no obvious source
  • The sound of running water audible when everything is off
